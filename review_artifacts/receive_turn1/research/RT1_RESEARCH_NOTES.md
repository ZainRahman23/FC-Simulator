Compiled 19 Oct 2026 for Touchline (authored receive-and-turn action library, organised by turn angle).

## 1. Receiving on the back foot (foot farther from the passer)

- **Definition.** It is the foot "furthest from where the pass is coming from and closest to where the next pass needs to go" [S18]. FIFA's circuit simply has "A plays a pass to B's back foot" [S1]. The FA lists back foot, front foot and half-turn as distinct receiving options [S17].
- **Why: it opens the body to the next action.** England Football says players should "receive on their back foot" so they "open their body out" to see the next player they want to pass to [S9]. FIFA says the receiver should offer "at an angle to be able to receive on the back foot, facilitating playing forward more quickly" [S1].
- **Ball path.** The ball is received on the back foot "allowing the ball to roll across the body", which carries it forward so the next action is also forward [S18]. McGuinness (Man Utd academy) lists "let the ball run across your body, take it late, take it early" among the receiving variations [S16].
- **Avoiding a separate turn.** The open-body back-foot receive lets the player play forward "without the need to turn"; a player facing their own goal "would need to turn so that they are facing the opposition's goal" [S18].
- **The passer's part.** The pass is aimed at the back foot [S1, S19, S34], and the receiver "need[s] to open their body early to indicate to their team-mate that they want the ball to be played to their back foot" [S18].
- **Terminology warning.** FIFA [S5] uses "front foot" for the foot "the furthest from pressure" (context: pressure from behind or the side). So "back/front" is defined relative to the passer in [S18, S1] but relative to the pressure in [S5]. A simulation should define the receiving foot geometrically (relative to ball line, exit direction and pressure), not by these labels **(inference)**.

## 2. Open body / half-turn / side-on orientation before the ball arrives

- **What it is.** The half-turn means "half of the players body is turned toward open play" [S32, tier C]. FIFA uses "side-on" and "open body shape" [S4, S5]. Coaches Insider: "Body position half turned to receive moving the ball on the first touch (changing direction)." [S20].
- **Why.** Peacock: "maintain an open body posture when receiving the ball" and "play on the half-turn" to see more of the pitch, keep control and play into space [S3]. England Football: "An open body shape encourages moving forwards with the ball to attack opponents" [S13]; the session focus is "opening up their body when receiving the ball" so players can see the passer and scan [S15]. One FA prompt: "can you see the ball, and can the ball see your feet?" [S38].
- **Orientation is both signal and enabler.** Dünker: "A receiving player's body orientation signals where they wish for the pass to be played and determines their ability to keep the ball under close control"; opening the body tells team-mates which foot and whether to feet or into the path; players must be aware of orientation "to allow them to move in the direction they wish to go" [S2].
- **Timing of preparation.**
  - The receiver opens "early", before the pass is made [S18].
  - Scanning happens before receiving [S1, S3, S4, S9, S10], and England Football asks for scanning "before, during, and after receiving the ball" [S12].
  - Research: the 180° trap study found advanced players "tended to rotate their bodies and pelvis toward the intended direction of movement" and "pre-positioned their feet to support this motion", "anticipating directional changes and preparing their movements in advance" [S27].
  - **No source quantifies how many ms before contact** the hips or shoulders should open. Sources say only before the pass, before the ball arrives, or in advance.
- **Scanning research.**
  - Definition of a scan: an active head movement where the face is "temporarily directed away from the ball" [S25].
  - Players with higher head-turn frequency in the 1 s or 2 s before possession "were more likely to turn with the ball", and higher head-turn excursion went with turning "for all time-periods" [S26]. The ball-turn code was "After receiving the ball, the player turns with the ball in order to move in a different direction", with **no angle threshold** [S26].
  - Scans are brief (about 0.4 s average) and rarely fixate on detail [S8]. Scanning is lower under high pressure, "relatively low in situations where the opponent pressure is high" [S25].
  - Prior work cited in [S25]: youth midfielders who scanned "performed more turns when opportunities arose".
- **Tracking definition (analytics).** PFF measures orientation "at the point the pass is played & at the point they receive the ball" and classifies receptions as opening (rotating toward the attacking direction), closing, or opening to goal. No degree thresholds are published [S23].

## 3. Receiving across the body

- The back-foot receive is inherently across the body: the ball "roll[s] across the body" [S18]; McGuinness: "let the ball run across your body" [S16].
- Being "two-sided" means being able to "receive and open up on both sides". Cochrane: "turning both ways is important, being able to open out on your right foot and open out on your left foot" [S16].
- Pressure constraint (tier C): the open-body back-foot turn "Requires awareness of no immediate pressure behind" [S33]. Tier B states the same: scanning is "particularly important when receiving to play forward", to check for opponents directly behind [S18].
- **(inference)** For a stationary receiver initially square to the passer, "across the body" only exists once the hips have opened. The pre-contact rotation turns the exit-side foot into the back foot, and the ball then travels past the near foot to the far (back) foot.

## 4. Directional first touch, touch distance and weight, "out of the feet"

- **Direction.** "The player must take a touch in the direction they intend to go" [S4]. "The first touch should be out of the feet and directed slightly forward" [S4]. "a good first touch out of the feet" and "quality first touch away from oncoming opponents" [S3]. Touch "away from the direction the defender jumps out to press" [S4]. First touch forward and away from pressure [S5]. The first touch "sets them up to either play a pass, take a shot on goal or shift the ball into space" [S2].
- **Weight and distance.** "A firmer touch, for example, will knock the ball further in front of a player". To drive forward, "their first touch should be away from them"; to dribble past a defender the ball should "remain closer to the player" [S18]. [S34] asks only "Close control, Into space or away from opponent?" and gives no rule.
- **Cushioning.** "A softer touch can be achieved by cushioning the ball" [S18]. Biomechanics: a side-foot stop shows "a distinct draw back of the foot before ball contact" after a gentle forward swing [S30]. The easy2coach receive says "The foot moves toward the incoming ball" and "Just before contact, the foot is pulled back (slightly slower than the ball speed)" [S24].
- **Turn-oriented trap (research).** Advanced players in the 180° trap absorbed momentum with "a quick outward rotation of the ankles" and had "more accurate alignment of their feet centers with the ball" [S27].
- **Moving vs stationary.** "Players should move onto the pass rather than receiving in a stationary position" to generate a firmer touch [S2].

## 5. Turning with inside / outside; spin; drop of the shoulder / fake

- **Surfaces.** FIFA prioritises "the inside of the foot when appropriate" [S4]. Corneal trains inside-foot turns, outside-foot turn-backs, sole drag-backs, sole turns and Cruyffs on both feet, using "all surfaces of the boot" [S7]. McGuinness: "receive inside and outside of the foot" [S16].
- **Outside of the foot in match play (TSG).**
  - Players had their "lower body orientated to move left but using the outside of the right foot" (the touch goes the other way, as a disguise).
  - Segura used "a clever first touch with the outside of her left foot" to roll the defender.
  - Oboavwoduo took "her first touch to the outside right of her body, rolling the defender" [S6].
- **Spin.**
  - Coaches Insider: the receiver "backs into defender and receives ball to feet, spin defender using their body as a screen"; also "Turn away with outside / inside of each foot." [S20].
  - Dünker: correct orientation and short, sharp movements let players "spin away from opposing players" [S2].
  - England Football: a striker "may need a tight turning circle to spin off a defender" [S11].
  - Tier C: "Shoulders rotate first, ball follows" [S33].
- **Drop of the shoulder / fake.**
  - Gravenberch uses "a drop of the shoulder or a knock and run" [S21].
  - The TSG describes a "decoy body shape and movement to evade pressure" [S6].
  - Coaches Insider: use the upper body "to create an element of surprise or disguise" [S20].
  - Corneal sells the Cruyff with a fake call [S7].
  - Deception is one of the six "core capabilities" of turning [S11].

## 6. One-motion large turns vs receive → stop → turn

- **Stationary turning is the problem case.** Dünker: "If players are stationary and wish to turn quickly, they may not be able to generate the speed or possesses the efficiency to do so without losing the ball" [S2]. This bears directly on the RT-1 brief (a stationary receiver).
- **One-motion examples.**
  - "This sequence should be one fluid movement … It is done with one ball touch" (inside-foot redirect behind the standing leg, then chase) [S24].
  - "Check off, receive and turn inside and face up to defender in one movement off first touch"; "check back into space you have created to receive and turn in one touch" [S20].
  - Gravenberch: "receiving the ball on the turn, a simple swivel of the hips and transfer of balance from one foot to another" [S22], and "opening his body up with his control" [S22].
  - "receives the ball on the turn and moves away" [S2].
- **Commit to rotation before or during contact (research).** Advanced players rotate the pelvis toward the intended direction during the trap and pre-position the feet [S27]. The release describes ball-trapping as "the act of stopping a soccer ball and changing direction", one action rather than stop-then-turn [S27].
- **Touch-count argument (tier C).** Square-on, a player usually needs "two or three touches" to turn; on the half-turn, "one simple first touch" [S32].
- **Zero-touch (no-touch) turns** are a recognised category [S11; also progression lists in S34]. See topic 7.
- **When not to turn.** If tightly marked, play back (FIFA variations: shadowed receiver plays back, or one-two) [S1]. Under pressure: hold the defender off with an arm and use turns and feints [S3]. Safe-side turning waits until the opponent "commits to a shoulder" before "rolling them to face forwards" [S21].

## 7. Letting the ball run across / through the stance; far-foot receives behind the standing leg

- **Let it run plus pivot (match evidence).**
  - Couto "opens her body with a 90-degree pivot to her right, letting the ball run past her" after a decoy closed body shape [S6].
  - Vera Jones let the ball run, then, seeing "both of her opponent's feet are planted", "opts to take her first touch inside" [S6].
  - McGuinness: "let the ball run across your body, take it late, take it early" [S16].
  - The "no-touch" turn is named by England Football [S11]. Mechanics are not described there; tier-C pages list it only as a progression [S34].
- **Inside-foot redirect behind / through the standing leg.**
  - The player "redirects the ball with the right inside through the legs", "behind the left standing leg to the back or side".
  - Coaching points: one touch, one fluid movement, foot pulled back just before contact, upper body upright, knees bent, don't redirect too firmly.
  - The turn: "Depending on pass and touch accuracy, the player makes a turn between 90 and 180 degrees" [S24].
  - The direction of body rotation is **not stated**. **(inference)** Ball behind the left leg implies a left (counter-clockwise from above) turn toward the ball.
- **Cruyff (receiving context).**
  - FIFA describes the Cruyff only as a ball-manipulation drill: "wrap the foot around the ball", perform with the right foot then push away with the same foot [S7].
  - Tier-C catalogue: drag the ball "behind the standing leg with the inside of the foot" after a fake [S33].
  - None of the sources read describes using a Cruyff as a first-touch receive of an incoming pass.
- **Gravenberch-style far-foot inside receive behind the standing leg.** No source read describes this specific action. The Gravenberch sources describe hip swivel and weight transfer [S22], safe-side turns, rolling the opponent, holding off with the upper body, and dropping the shoulder [S21]. The closest documented mechanic is [S24].

## 8. Hook turns, drag-back / sole turns, Cruyff: when used and what geometry they suit

- **Listed turns.** England Football: one-touch, no-touch, stop turn, inside hook, outside hook, drag back, stepover, Cruyff [S11]. Cruyff, outside hook and drag-back are suggested "to escape any interference", with "keeping the ball close to their body when they receive and turn" [S12].
- **Inside hook.** Inside of the foot "over and behind the ball", rotating and lowering the body, dragging the ball the opposite way [S35, tier C]. Tier C also calls it a 180° pivot protecting the ball "with the turning shoulder" [S33].
- **Outside hook.** Outside of the foot "over and behind the ball", standing knee bent, ball taken the opposite way [S35]. SoccerHelp: toes up, outside of foot hooks, stops and pulls back [S36].
- **Drag-back / pullback.**
  - Sole on the ball, pull it back "in the direction you came from"; "a good way to make a 180 degree turn to go the opposite way" [S36].
  - Step-by-step: slow, "place standing leg beside the ball", weight through the standing leg, drag with the studs, keep it within about 1 m, turn and go [S37].
  - FIFA drills drag-back and sole turns on both feet [S7].
- **Sole 180° half-turn biomechanics (dribbling, not receiving).** Faster turns involved "short steps, limited lateral and forward body movement, a lowered centre of mass, abducted arms, and restricted trunk flexion and pelvic inclination". Fast and slow trials differed in support-side hip rotation, driving-side hip adduction, and trunk flexion and rotation [S28; paraphrase of abstract].
- **Speed context (tier C).** "When going really fast, a Stop/Turn is a good way to turn"; hooks and pullbacks suit lower speed [S36].
- **Geometry, ball reversed vs ball continues (inference).**
  - The coaching sources describe hooks, drag-backs and Cruyffs mainly in a **dribbling** context, where they reverse the ball relative to the player's own travel [S7, S35, S36, S37].
  - For a **stationary receiver facing the passer**, the incoming ball already travels toward the receiver's back. So turning to face away from the passer needs little or no reversal of the ball (let it run, redirect behind the standing leg, open across the body). A hook or drag-back is what you need when the ball has to come back toward the passer's side, or after the ball has been stopped and the player then turns.
  - No source states this geometry explicitly.

## 9. Preparation before contact: support foot, stepping away, presenting the foot, shoulders, checking away

- **Create an angle and move off the line.**
  - "offer for the ball at an angle" [S1]; "B pulls wide of the cone to receive on the half-turn" [S1].
  - "must create a good angle to receive the pass" [S10]; "create an angle between themselves and the server" [S19]; support from a diagonal, not a straight line [S20].
- **Step away / check away, then show.**
  - "take a step in the opposite direction before receiving the pass" (to deceive) [S1].
  - Dünker: the receiver "takes a small step to the side to open up an 'exit route'", simulating drawing in a defender and creating space to drive into [S2].
  - "an in and out movement away from the cone as if they are trying to deceive a defender" [S14].
  - "Receiver move to the ball … or away to create space to come back into"; "Run defender off and away from the ball then check back into space you have created" [S20].
  - "Being blindside of the defender can give a critical advantage" [S10].
- **Readiness.** "Being on their toes and timing their movements allows players to adjust their feet more quickly in readiness to receive a pass" [S2]. "keep your knees bent for good balance" [S20].
- **Presenting the receiving foot.** In the TSG analysis González "opens up the orientation of her right (receiving) foot" and touches into the space behind [S6]. Corneal: hip movement and wrapping the body around the ball to turn quickly [S7].
- **Support foot.**
  - Advanced players "pre-positioned their feet to support this motion" [S27].
  - Drag-back: standing leg beside the ball, weight through it [S37].
  - Hooks: standing knee bent; place the non-kicking foot well; manage distance to the ball (paraphrased coaching points) [S35, tier C].
  - No source gives a precise support-foot placement relative to the ball for a receive-and-turn.
- **Arms and shielding.**
  - Outstretched arm "to gauge the press" [S1]; an arm to hold off an opponent [S3].
  - "Get your body between the ball and your opponent" [S20]; holding off "with his upper body" [S21].
  - Tier C: "keep the nearest shoulder between the defender and the ball" [S33].
  - Research: faster sole half-turns had abducted arms [S28].
- **Scanning before the pass:** see topic 2 [S1, S8, S9, S25, S26].

## 10. Why different turn angles favour different mechanics

- **Coaching sources do not give an angle-based selection rule.** The closest qualitative statements:
  - The open-body back-foot receive avoids a turn when the goal is to play forward [S18].
  - Square-on reception needs extra touches to turn [S32, tier C].
  - Hooks, drag-backs and Cruyffs are described as reversal tools, a "180 degree turn to go the opposite way" [S36], typically against pressure or in tight space [S11, S12, S35].
  - The behind-the-standing-leg redirect spans 90-180° [S24].
  - Positional framing: a striker spins with a tight turning circle; a full-back opens the body to switch play [S11].
- **Match descriptions with angles:** a "90-degree pivot" letting the ball run [S6]; 180° directional traps [S27]; 180° sole half-turns [S28].
- **General change-of-direction science (no ball) [S29].** The review reports angle-dependent techniques:
  - The penultimate foot contact has "a limited role … when changing direction ≤ 45°" and "a prominent role for CODs ≥ 60°".
  - Crossover cut for subtle changes ("greater velocity maintenance"); sidestep for sharp cuts.
  - A pivot is "a bilateral turning strategy where one foot rotates and remains in contact with the ground (typically for directional changes ≥ 135°)".
  - "greater pelvic rotation was demonstrated during the 90° cut" (vs 45°).
  - "sharper CODs result in reduced approach velocities and exit velocities".
  - "the executed COD angle is smaller than the intended COD angle".
  - These are running cuts with approach velocity, not stationary receptions with a ball.
- **(inference)** Two variables plausibly drive mechanic choice:
  - (a) how far the **body** must rotate (≈ exit angle from the initial facing);
  - (b) how far the **ball** must be redirected (≈ 180° − exit angle, because the pass travels toward the receiver's back).
  - Small exits need a large ball redirection (cushion and push back or sideways off the near/front foot). Exits near 180° need little ball redirection but a large body rotation (let it run, across-body back-foot receive, behind-the-standing-leg redirect, pivot).
  - This is a geometric derivation, not a source claim.

## 11. Which foot (near/far, front/back) and which surface for which direction; dominant-foot effects

- **Back foot = the foot "closest to where the next pass needs to go"** [S18]. So for a forward or away exit, the receiving foot is the one on the exit side, after opening [S1, S9, S18]. **(inference)** Turning out to the right means receiving with the right (back) foot after opening the hips to the right. This is mirrored for the left.
- **Front foot (FIFA, pressure-relative):** pass to the foot "the furthest from pressure" to allow a forward, protective first touch [S5].
- **Surface options documented:**
  - Inside of the foot (FIFA's default "when appropriate") [S4].
  - Outside of the foot on the exit side, including as a disguise against the lower-body orientation [S6].
  - Inside of the far foot redirecting behind the standing leg [S24].
  - Sole for stop / drag / reverse [S7, S36, S37].
  - Wrap of the foot around the ball for the Cruyff [S7].
- **Surface by direction.** No source read gives a rule mapping surface to direction of turn. Coaches Insider allows either surface: "Turn away with outside / inside of each foot." [S20].
- **Dominant foot.**
  - World Cup '98 players were heavily one-footed: "first touches, passes, dribbles and tackles were rarely performed with the non-preferred foot". Yet they were "as skilled, on average, with their non-preferred foot … on the rare occasions when they used it" [S31].
  - Coaching aims for "two-sided" rather than "two-footed" [S16]. Drills are run on both feet and in both directions [S7, S35].
  - Dünker's drill deliberately controls with one foot and shoots with the other to keep movements short [S2].
  - **(inference)** A left/right-mirrored library should still bias selection toward the preferred foot, with a non-preferred penalty that shows mostly as selection frequency rather than execution quality (per [S31]).

## Explicit angle guidance in the sources

**There is essentially none for receive-and-turn mechanic selection.** Every number found:
- **[S24]** The inside-foot redirect behind the standing leg results in "a turn between 90 and 180 degrees", depending on pass and touch accuracy. This is a description of outcome range, not a selection boundary.
- **[S6]** One clip shows a "90-degree pivot to her right, letting the ball run past her". Descriptive only.
- **[S36]** Pullback / drag-back = "180 degree turn" (dribbling). [S33, tier C] calls the inside hook a 180° pivot.
- **[S27]** The study examined only a 180° directional trap. **[S28]** The half-turn is a 180° change of direction with the sole (dribbling).
- **[S29]** General change of direction without the ball, not football receiving: the ≤45° / ≥60° role of the penultimate foot contact; the pivot strategy "typically" ≥135°; more pelvic rotation at 90° than at 45°. These are the only published angle boundaries found, and they describe running cuts.
- **[S26]** The "turn with the ball" code has **no angle threshold**. **[S23]** PFF open/close-body metrics have **no published thresholds**.
- No FIFA, FA or other coaching source states angle bands for when to use a back-foot receive, a let-it-run, a hook, a drag-back or a Cruyff. Any degree boundaries in the Touchline library must be **project design decisions** (calibrated in our own tests or from video), not attributed to these sources.

## Mechanics summary relevant to a simulation

| # | Neutral requirement | Status |
|---|---|---|
| M1 | **Information before contact.** The receiver has scanned before the pass arrives. More head-turn frequency in the last 1-2 s, and more excursion, go with a higher likelihood of turning [S26]. In an authored action this is a precondition (exit target known) plus visible head checks before the ball arrives. | Research-supported (S25, S26, S1, S9, S12) |
| M2 | **Body opens before contact.** Hips/pelvis (and shoulders) rotate toward the intended exit before the ball arrives ("open their body early", before the pass [S18]). Advanced players rotate the pelvis toward the intended direction during the trap and pre-position the feet [S27]. | Research- and coaching-supported. **Exact timing (ms before contact) not in any source** |
| M3 | **A stationary start is a handicap.** Turning quickly from stationary may lack speed or efficiency [S2]. The action should therefore begin with a preparatory movement (step aside to open an exit route [S2], step the opposite way or pull wide [S1], in-and-out [S14]) or at least a weight shift onto the support foot. | Coaching-supported (S1, S2, S14, S20); application to stationary start = inference |
| M4 | **Receiving foot depends on exit side and ball side.** For forward or away exits, receive on the back foot, which after opening is the exit-side foot. The ball travels across the body from the near side to that foot [S1, S9, S18, S16]. Under pressure from one side, receive on the foot furthest from pressure [S5]. | Coaching-supported; geometric mapping to left/right turn = inference |
| M5 | **First-touch direction = exit direction**, out of the feet, weight matched to the next action (firmer and further to drive, closer to dribble or under pressure) [S4, S3, S18, S2]. | Coaching-supported |
| M6 | **Contact is cushioned.** The foot moves toward the ball, then draws back just before contact [S30, S24]. For turning traps, ankle outward rotation and foot-centre alignment with the ball [S27]. | Research-supported (S30, S27); S24 coaching |
| M7 | **One continuous action for large turns.** Receive and rotate together ("one fluid movement" [S24]; hip swivel and weight transfer [S22]; "in one movement off first touch" [S20]; trap = stop and change direction together [S27]). Avoid authored receive → stop → turn as the default for a prepared receiver. | Coaching- and research-supported (qualitative) |
| M8 | **Ball continues vs ball reversed.** For a receiver facing the passer, ball redirection ≈ 180° − exit angle and body rotation ≈ exit angle. Near-180° exits suit let-it-run, behind-the-standing-leg or across-body receives with a big body pivot [S6, S24, S16]. Exits that need the ball to come back or sideways suit cushion-and-redirect with the inside or outside, or sole/hook mechanics [S7, S35, S36, S37]. | Geometry = inference; mechanics per source |
| M9 | **Zero-touch option.** The let-it-run plus pivot is a legitimate receive: the body pivots while the ball passes, and the first touch comes after [S6, S11, S16]. It needs space and no immediate pressure behind [S18; S33]. | Coaching- and match-analysis-supported |
| M10 | **Disguise layer (optional).** The lower body is set one way while the touch goes the other with the outside of the opposite foot [S6]; drop of the shoulder [S21]; fakes [S7]; decoy closed shape before opening [S6]. | Coaching- and match-analysis-supported |
| M11 | **Pressure gating.** With tight pressure from behind: shield (body between ball and opponent, arm), receive on the safe side, wait for the defender to commit a shoulder, then roll; or play back [S1, S3, S20, S21, S6]. The open-body turn is for when there is no immediate pressure behind [S18]. | Coaching-supported |
| M12 | **Sharper turns cost speed.** Sharper changes of direction reduce exit velocity, increase braking demand on the step before the plant, increase pelvic rotation, and shift toward a pivot strategy at very sharp angles. The executed angle tends to undershoot the intended one [S29]. Fast sole 180° turns use short steps, a lowered COM and abducted arms [S28]. | Research-supported for general CoD and dribbling half-turns; transfer to stationary receive = inference |
| M13 | **Footedness.** Real players use the preferred foot for most first touches [S31], but coaching aims for opening out on both sides [S16]. A mirrored library should carry a preferred-foot selection bias. | Research-supported (S31); library policy = inference |
| M14 | **Angle bands are ours to define.** No source provides degree boundaries for choosing between these mechanics (see the section above). | Stated honestly |
