# Preserve momentum while making support legible

**Real football evidence supports different movement costs for shallow corrections, hard cuts and true reversals; it does not supply a numerical ceiling for AGILITY-99-V1.** Shallow cuts can retain substantial speed, whereas sharper changes demand more braking and different support mechanics. A direct reversal must remove opposed motion before establishing speed back along the new line. Across these actions, preparation, support transfer and propulsion should explain the centre-of-mass path. This supports a responsive prototype that distinguishes momentum handling from body presentation, rather than requiring every request to wait through one prescribed plant. The following engineering implications are explicitly design inferences: the studies mostly use preplanned, ball-free drills, not surprise joystick input or elite dribbling.

## Shallow corrections should preserve useful speed

In twelve young soccer players completing 20 m sprints, a 90° change produced greater deceleration and lower minimum speed than a 45° change. Maintaining speed through the change was associated with faster overall performance. The verified abstract supports **angle-dependent speed loss**, not a universal near-stop requirement. It does not establish the best possible 15°, 30° or 60° performance. ([Hader, Palazzi and Buchheit, 2015](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/clanak/206957))

A separate study of 25 experienced soccer players found different performance predictors at 45° and 90°. Hip extension/sagittal power and lateral centre-of-mass to centre-of-pressure separation mattered at 45°; lateral ground-reaction impulse and frontal-plane hip power mattered at 90°. This strengthens the case for distinct movement strategies rather than one turn animation scaled by angle. ([Havens and Sigward, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25102291/))

The design inference is to preserve velocity useful for the requested path while removing incompatible motion. Judge a correction by minimum speed and useful exit speed as well as heading response. For repeated small corrections, FIFA coaching describes fluid footwork, body movements and movement into opening space; it supplies no mandatory pause between adjustments. Retargetable 15–30° corrections are therefore a reasonable candidate to test, but their response time remains a gameplay choice. ([FIFA Training Centre, 2024](https://www.fifatrainingcentre.com/en/practice/elite-sessions/in-possession/mastering-ball-control.php))

## Hard cuts need braking without compulsory stops

A study of 27 men comparing 45°, 90° and 180° changes found lower approach, contact and exit velocities and longer ground contact as angle increased. Trunk, pelvis and foot behaviour also differed. This supports greater cost for sharper cuts, but its abstract supplies no exact elite speed or yaw bound for the prototype. ([Dos’Santos, Thomas and Jones, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34278968/))

CUTtheACL analysed **6,008 preplanned 90° cuts from 1,002 football academy players**. It distinguished braking/load acceptance and propulsion within the cut stance. Mean contact time was 317 ± 58 ms, with load acceptance averaging 75 ± 42 ms. These describe a particular, mostly adolescent cohort approaching from 5 m in running shoes on artificial turf. They must not become a 317 ms gameplay delay or universal contact minimum. ([Di Paolo et al., 2025](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ksa.70017))

For AGILITY-99-V1, prompt braking and redirection can coexist with a meaningful speed dip. A compulsory near-zero speed at every 90–120° request is not demonstrated by this evidence. The exact balance between curvature, braking and retained speed must come from the prototype comparisons.

## Reversals must cancel opposed momentum

In a 180° test with twenty university male soccer players, the third-last contact produced greater peak braking forces in less contact time than subsequent contacts. Greater braking and more horizontally directed forces across the approach contacts were associated with faster performance. **The final plant does not perform all the braking.** ([Dos’Santos, Thomas and Jones, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33377421/))

The engineering inference is to count removal of negative velocity along the requested exit as braking, then use independent acceleration to build positive exit speed. This preserves a genuine reversal cost without adding an idle pause at zero. A rounded reversal can retain useful sideways motion; entering a “reversal” category need not force zero total speed. The papers do not validate immediate simulation-step response or any chosen braking constant. Those are design decisions, and must remain separate from claims about human reaction time.

## Support should explain the path across steps

An adolescent soccer intervention coached penultimate-step braking, a lower centre of mass, a final-foot push toward the exit and trunk inclination. Selected kinematics and final-foot contact time improved, but overall performance and trunk inclination did not improve significantly. The coaching cues are useful visual references, not proof that a particular animation increases performance. ([Nakamura et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1453859/full))

Cross-sport evidence from ten American football players performing 45° cuts also found redirection distributed across approach, execution and following steps. This supports readable preparation, a supporting contact and follow-through, rather than a frozen body followed by a root swivel. It is not association-football dribbling evidence. ([Yamashita et al., 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1576614/full))

## Conclusion

The candidate should be assessed on two separate questions: does its momentum response serve the requested action, and can its body/support system explain that response? A good movement result with poor body catch-up identifies presentation work; it does not alone justify reducing the movement ceiling. Likewise, a visually convincing plant cannot excuse unnecessary delay. No retrieved evidence establishes an exact torso lag, yaw cap, input latency or elite dribbling maximum: those remain explicit prototype choices.
