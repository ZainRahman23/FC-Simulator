1. Does reducing non-stud body friction materially increase slide distance? Yes. The COM path from ground entry to stop is:
    • F100: 1.80 m
    • F75: 2.39 m
    • F50: 3.86 m
    • F25: 8.86 m
    • F10: ≥ 19.6 m, still moving at 6 s
    • F0: ≥ 28.1 m, still moving at 6 s
    F0 does not decelerate at all after ground entry (v_h stays 5.03 m/s).
  2. Is the relationship approximately monotonic? Yes for distance, and for the time to lose 50% / 90% of the entry speed:
    • F100: 0.24 / 0.64 s
    • F75: 0.39 / 0.95 s
    • F50: 0.78 / 1.57 s
    • F25: 1.77 / 3.38 s
    • F10: 4.83 s / not reached
    • F0: neither reached
    It is not monotonic for "stop time" or for the entry → stop average deceleration (F100: 2.49 s and 2.26 m/s²; F75: 1.93 s and 3.20; F50: 2.16 s and 2.78). F100 has shed 90% of its speed by 0.64 s, then creeps at 0.2–0.3 m/s for about 1.5 s while it rolls over, which stretches its stop time.
  3. Does the tackle begin to visually "glide" rather than grab the turf? Partly.
    • F100 grabs: half the speed is gone 0.24 s after ground entry.
    • F50 and F25 visibly keep sliding seated for longer.
    • But the lower the friction, the sooner the body rolls past its side onto its front: F25 at 1.17 s, F10 at 1.12 s, F0 at 1.07 s, against F100 at 2.31 s. Much of the extra distance is covered lying face-down, not as a clean seated glide.
  4. Which body regions currently produce most of the braking? F100, ground entry → stop, 383 N·s in total:
    • knee 189 N·s (50%)
    • thigh 78 (20%)
    • boots on heel/upper/side 69 (18%): the tackling boot's heel and upper about 49, the folded boot's upper about 23
    • torso 28 (7%)
    • shin/calf 17 (4%)
    • hip/pelvis about 0
    • arms about 0
    • forearms/hands 5 (1%)
    The braking is concentrated in the first 0.6 s (4.78 → 0.69 m/s), while the knee, thigh and boots are down. Each of them slides at the full Coulomb ratio (braking/normal ≈ 0.5).
  5. Are hands/forearms significant brakes in F100? No: 4.8 N·s of braking (1%) for 61 N·s of normal impulse (3%), with upper arms about 0. The hands have the highest non-boot μ in the spec (0.7), but they touch mostly late, at low speed.
  6. Is the knee's ~50% load share responsible for substantial braking, or mainly normal support? It is the main brake. Over the slide the knee carries 24% of the normal impulse (435 of 1845 N·s) but does 50% of the braking. It is the region on the turf during the fast phase right after entry, braking at 0.43 per unit of normal load (μ 0.5).
    The hip/pelvis is the opposite case: 27% of the normal impulse (505 N·s) and about 0% of the braking, because it is loaded mostly after the body has already slowed. Load share and braking share differ because of timing.
  7. At what friction range does the slide begin to resemble the reference? Around ×0.5 (F50; body μ ≈ 0.25, hands 0.35).
    • Its mean deceleration over the first second after entry is 3.1 m/s². The reference's lying phase is 2.5–3.3 m/s², μ_eff 0.26–0.34, on a pitch that looks wet.
    • Its seated sliding phase lasts until about 1.2 s.
    • F75 still sheds half its speed in 0.39 s.
    • No condition ≥ F50 reproduces the reference's first 0.25 s of knee-glide with almost no speed loss: the landing itself costs F50 1.3 m/s in its first 0.25 s (the landing's normal impulse × μ).
  8. At what point does it become obviously too slippery? F25 and below.
    • Mean deceleration: 1.3 m/s² (F25), 0.5 (F10), 0 (F0).
    • One second after entry the body still moves at 3.4 m/s (F25), 4.4 m/s (F10) and 5.0 m/s (F0): ice-like.
    • F25 slides 8.9 m from a 5 m/s entry. The reference covers 7.5 m from 9 m/s before getting up.
  9. Does lowering body friction expose another braking mechanism? No.
    • On flat turf only contact tangential impulses can change the COM's horizontal momentum. The ledger closes against M·Δv_h to ≤ 0.19 N·s in every condition.
    • At F0 the horizontal speed stays at 5.03 m/s for 5.6 s.
    • Muscles, joint limits, impact redirection and the solver contribute nothing measurable to horizontal braking.
    What lowering friction does expose is rotational: the body rolls onto its front earlier (prone at 2.31 s in F100, 1.07 s in F0), and the fold-hip limit excursion grows (3.0° → 10.0°).
  10. Do the tackling studs remain correctly disengaged in every normal run? Yes. Neither boot engages its studs after the plant push-off in any condition (stud-engaged normal impulse 0 N·s for both boots). The tackling boot touches only on its heel, upper or side (220–270 N·s of normal impulse over the run), and those contacts belong to the scaled group.
  11. Does any condition introduce instability, pathological rotation or joint-limit problems?
    • No numerical instability: all six ran the full 6 s, finite and deterministic.
    • Pathological rotation: yes, in every condition, F100 included. F100 rolls onto its front at 2.31 s, just after SP2A's 2 s evaluation window, which corrects the SP2A report. It happens earlier as friction drops.
    • Fold-hip constraint excursion grows: 3.0° (F100), 5.0, 7.1, 9.0, 9.2, 10.0° (F0), each for about 1.1–1.3 s.
    • Peak hip/pelvis load rises from 1.3 to 5.2 BW (impacts while rolling).
    • The tackling-ankle excursion shrinks (2.5° → 1.6°).
  12. Based solely on this diagnostic, is non-stud body/turf friction the principal remaining cause of insufficient slide distance? Yes.
    • The slide's braking is entirely non-stud body/turf friction (body μ 0.5, hands 0.7, boot upper 0.5), acting at the knee, thigh and boot uppers during the landing phase.
    • The studs play no part after the push-off, and removing that friction removes all of the deceleration.
    • Whether 0.5 is the right body|turf value is a separate calibration question: the reference suggests μ_eff ≈ 0.3 on a likely wet pitch.
    • Lowering friction alone does not give a clean glide, because the roll onto the front (present at F100 too) arrives sooner.
